WHAT I DO — SIX AREAS, OPEN ANY FOR THE EVIDENCE

01AI ENGINEERING

Building language‑model systems, and knowing when they are lying.

Evaluation first. Generating fluent text is the easy half; proving it is faithful to the evidence is the half that decides whether anyone can use it.

Most of my AI work is the unglamorous part — the gate that decides whether generated output is good enough to put in front of a client, and the benchmark that says how often it is right.

The evaluation gate

Sole designer and implementer of the pipeline every AI persona passes before it reaches a client. It searches a vector store for the ground‑truth evidence behind each claim, puts the pair to an ensemble of five judges, and uses agreement between them as a confidence score. Where the judges disagree, the claim goes to a person rather than being averaged away.

Validation went from one to two days of two analysts’ time to 15–30 minutes. 300–500 claims per project, across five to eight personas.

Knowing the number means something

A held‑out benchmark scoring persona fidelity against real answers the system had never seen, with the match criterion defined separately for each data type, and the judges themselves calibrated against a 50‑pair reference set before being trusted.

88–90% alignment on held‑out data.

Multi‑granularity retrieval — built and tested, not deployed

This one is a prototype, not a shipped feature. It is a retrieval router that chooses how coarsely to chunk a document for each individual query instead of fixing one chunk size for everything: hierarchical chunking across six levels, labels generated by cosine similarity rather than by hand, and a small PyTorch network trained to predict the right granularity. Complete and working, but not integrated into the live product and not yet benchmarked against the fixed‑chunk system it would replace.

~56% top‑1 and ~72% top‑2 granularity accuracy in testing. Adapts a published method; the decision to apply it, and the design, build and testing, are mine.

Making the data embeddable in the first place

Around 17,000 question–answer pairs generated from interview transcripts to train that router, and a transformation that turns tabular client data into natural language a model can actually read — including the codebook that tells it what each variable means.

Agents measured on behaviour, not satisfaction

Co‑built and evaluated three conversational agents with around 300 real users, measuring pre/post change in intention rather than whether people enjoyed the conversation.

02STATISTICS AND EXPERIMENTS

Designing studies that can actually answer the question.

Randomised trials, mediation analysis, and the part afterwards where a result has to become a decision somebody acts on.

An experiment is only worth running if its result would change what you do. Most of my statistical work is in that design step, and in the translation back out to the people who have to act on it.

NHS lung health check invitations

First author on a five‑arm randomised experiment testing how the wording of an invitation letter affects whether people attend an NHS Targeted Lung Health Check. I ran the full analytical pipeline and translated the findings for clinical and policy stakeholders.

n = 3,274. Under review at Nature Communications; preprint on medRxiv.

Which patients respond to which doctors

A randomised experiment for a pharmaceutical company testing three communication styles across four patient types in two markets. One of two analysts on the project; I led the counterfactual mediation analysis — whether health literacy, emotion and trust in the doctor carry the effect — and presented the headline results to the client.

~60,000 participants screened, ~6,000 analysed, five research questions. The findings are the client’s and are not disclosed here.

The methods underneath

Logistic and covariate‑adjusted regression, counterfactual mediation, mixed‑effects models, bootstrapping, difference‑in‑differences and switchback designs, structural equation modelling, and exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis.

03MACHINE LEARNING

Finding the structure in data that does not line up.

Clustering, prediction and feature selection on multi‑source data that nobody designed to be joined together.

Real datasets rarely arrive in one shape. A lot of the difficulty is upstream of the model: deciding what can legitimately be compared before anything is clustered or predicted.

The segmentation engine behind the personas

Built the unsupervised pipeline that turns raw respondent data into the segments a product is built around. Respondents come from different sources answering similar but not identical questionnaires, so it reduces across sources with PCA first, derives segments with UMAP and HDBSCAN, and uses random forest for feature importance within and across datasets.

Validating a model somebody else built

Led the analysis validating and extending a client’s patient segmentation model for a population it had not been built on — linear discriminant analysis, confusion matrices, permutation tests. The client adopted the revised tool.

Prediction, compared honestly

Research lead on an AI‑powered psychometric prediction tool, comparing XGBoost, random forest, elastic net, a PyTorch neural network and plain regression on large psychometric datasets, rather than assuming the most complex model wins. Variable‑importance methods then steered the product toward a cheaper instrument.

Reinforcement learning — built, not shipped

A recommender using a multi‑armed bandit with Thompson sampling to personalise behavioural interventions, alongside causal discovery in R to learn Bayesian graphs from timestamped activity data. The engagement ended before it was deployed.

04BEHAVIOURAL SCIENCE

Designing for what people actually do.

The training underneath the modelling. It shows up as a constraint on the design, not as a coat of paint on the end.

Behavioural science is why I tend to ask what carried an effect rather than only whether there was one, and why I would rather measure a change in behaviour than a satisfaction score.

Where it shows up in the work

The NHS trial is a behavioural intervention — the thing being manipulated is the wording. The conversational agents were judged on whether intention moved, not on whether users liked them. Mediation analysis asks the behavioural question directly: not whether it worked, but what carried it.

An AI‑adoption diagnostic — at design stage

Leading the design of a diagnostic that segments employees by where they are on the AI adoption journey and which barriers are holding them back, so an organisation can target interventions instead of running the same training for everybody. Construct selection, a literature review of what predicts acceptance and use of generative AI, and a validation plan built on factor analysis and structural equation modelling.

At design stage. No results yet.

Training

MSc Behaviour Change at University College London, High Merit, with a dissertation carried out in collaboration with NHS England and NHS Digital.

06CONSULTING

Explaining a model to the people who have to act on it.

Architecture advice, client presentations, and turning repeated manual work into tooling the rest of the team can use.

Architecture advice

Making the work reusable

Productised repeated manual work — literature review, question–answer generation, labelling — into documented, version‑controlled pipelines the team can run without me, and led internal upskilling in agentic AI tooling.

Reporting that runs itself

Built a scheduled Microsoft Fabric pipeline — Python steps and Power Query over MySQL — refreshing eight times a day behind client‑facing Power BI reporting, and co‑presented it with the CEO. Introduced version‑controlled collaboration for the team and wrote the deployment and reproducibility documentation.
